# Pull the Pin / Ball Flow Puzzle

## Overview

A Rube Goldberg-style 2D physics puzzle where colored balls are held in place by a series of pins above matching colored cups. Players remove pins in sequence to route each ball into its correct cup. The challenge is ordering pin removals to avoid cross-contamination — wrong-colored balls in a cup fail the level.

## Game Mechanics

- **Setup:** A vertical 2D scene with colored balls held above by pins, colored cups below
- **Control:** Tap or drag to pull individual pins
- **Physics:** Gravity and flow simulation drives balls through channels once pins are removed
- **Win condition:** All balls land in matching-color cups
- **Fail condition:** Any ball lands in the wrong cup
- **Difficulty scaling:** More pins, more colors, more complex routing channels, timed variants

The mechanic is self-teaching — a player understands the goal in under two seconds by looking at the screen. No tutorial text is required.

## What Makes It Appealing

### Instant Legibility
The entire rule set communicates visually: colored ball, colored cup, pin in the way. Players grasp the problem and goal before any UI appears.

### The "Wrong Move" Hook
Most ads for this genre deliberately show an incorrect pin removal — the wrong ball falls into the wrong cup. This triggers **reactance**: the viewer's brain fires a correction impulse ("that's not right, you should pull *that* one"). The viewer downloads to prove they can do it correctly.

### The Curiosity Gap
Ads cut away before resolution, leaving an open loop. The brain treats unresolved puzzles as pending tasks (the Zeigarnik Effect) — the viewer feels a low-level compulsion to close the loop by playing.

### Near-Completion States
Ads often show a near-solved board — one pin away from the solution — which is more psychologically compelling than showing an unsolved board. The brain's completion circuitry activates when a goal is visibly within reach.

## Psychological Principles at Play

| Principle | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Reactance** (Brehm, 1966) | Watching the wrong choice made creates a drive to take control and do it correctly |
| **Zeigarnik Effect** | Unfinished puzzles occupy working memory more than completed ones — the open loop demands closure |
| **Curiosity Gap** (Loewenstein) | Partial information is more motivating than full information; the ad withholds resolution |
| **Completion Drive** | Near-complete visual states activate the same neural circuits as near-miss gambling rewards |
| **Fail Ad Psychology** | Deliberately showing failure cuts CPI by ~55% vs. showing success — failure activates competitive motivation more reliably |

## Notable Games

- **Pull the Pin** (Mobge Ltd.) — the canonical game; actually matches the advertised mechanic
- **Pin Puzzle: Pull the Pin** — direct genre clone
- **Hero Wars** (Nexters) — the most notorious *abuser*; uses pin-puzzle ads for what is actually an idle RPG/auto-battler
- **Homescapes / Gardenscapes** (Playrix) — match-3 decoration games that ran pin-pulling ads for years; UK's Advertising Standards Authority banned them in 2020 after Playrix admitted the pin mini-games appeared in fewer than 0.03% of player sessions
- **Evony: The King's Return** — city-building strategy game advertising pin puzzles
- **Royal Match** (Dream Games) — "King's Nightmare" mode shown in ads exists but is extremely rare in actual play

## Ad Authenticity

Pull the Pin is unusual in that the actual game genuinely matches the advertised mechanic — it is one of the few hyper-casual genres where downloading delivers what the ad promised. The bait-and-switch behavior comes from heavier games (RPGs, strategy titles, match-3) poaching the format's exceptional click-through rate for their own unrelated products.
