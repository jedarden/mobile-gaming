# Merge Games (Merge Mansion Style)

## Overview

A grid of items where players drag two identical items together to produce the next-tier item in an upgrade chain. The chain extends dozens of levels — two twigs merge into a branch, branches into a log, logs into lumber, lumber into a table. A renovation or story meta-layer sits above the merge board: completing merge chains generates resources used to restore rooms, mansions, or gardens. The dominant format is Merge-2 (pairs only).

## Game Mechanics

- **Setup:** A grid (typically 5×7 to 6×9) pre-populated with various-tier items; some cells are locked
- **Control:** Drag one item onto an identical item to merge; items must be the same tier
- **Output:** Each merge produces a single item one tier higher
- **Meta loop:** High-tier items are "spent" on renovation tasks shown in a separate story layer
- **Energy system:** Grid space is the primary constraint; energy gates in some implementations limit merges per session
- **Difficulty scaling:** Wider chains, locked cells, rare items that only appear from specific sources, multi-chain interdependencies

Most merge games include a narrative wrapper — a mystery, a renovation project, a character relationship — that provides context for why the player is merging objects.

## What Makes It Appealing

### Permanent Progress Visibility
Unlike match-3 where the board clears and resets, merge games leave permanent evidence of advancement. Every high-tier item on the board represents accumulated effort. Players can see their history in the grid.

### The Merge Mansion Grandmother Effect
Merge Mansion became notorious for its telenovela-style ads featuring a suspicious grandmother with a dark secret. This created a meta-curiosity about the storyline entirely separate from the gameplay — players downloaded to find out what the grandmother was hiding. The ads didn't just sell a game mechanic; they sold a narrative mystery.

### Variable Ratio Reinforcement
Merges sometimes produce better items than expected — a surprise bonus tier, a rare random item. This intermittent, unpredictable reward schedule is the same mechanism that makes slot machines compelling. The player can't know which merge will produce the surprise.

### Collection Completion Drive
The Endowment Effect for incomplete sets is well-documented: once someone starts a collection, incomplete sets create persistent discomfort. Merge games exploit this by making chain completions visible — players can see exactly how close they are to the next tier, making stopping difficult.

### Sunk Cost Architecture
The more time and in-app purchases a player invests, the harder quitting becomes. Merge Mansion has been described as "a masterclass in the sunk cost fallacy." Each session's tiny investment compounds into a large accumulated investment that justifies another session.

## Psychological Principles at Play

| Principle | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Variable ratio reinforcement** | Unpredictable merge bonuses mimic slot machine reward schedules — the most addictive reinforcement pattern known |
| **Sunk cost escalation** | Accumulated investment creates psychological barrier to quitting |
| **Collection completion drive** | Incomplete chains cause persistent discomfort; the Endowment Effect makes incomplete sets feel like a loss |
| **Progress visibility** | Permanent grid items function as visible monuments to past effort, unlike games with resetting boards |
| **Investment-reward treadmill** | Early merges are fast and cheap; later merges are slow and expensive, creating a progression that always feels just within reach |

## Ad Strategy

Merge game ads fall into two categories:
1. **Gameplay demos:** Show the satisfying cascade of a near-full board suddenly consolidating into a single high-tier item
2. **Narrative mystery:** Show the story wrapper (the suspicious grandmother, the mysterious renovation, the dramatic character) without gameplay — creating download desire through narrative curiosity

The narrative mystery approach is more sophisticated and typically targets older demographics (30–50) who respond to story hooks rather than pure mechanic hooks.
