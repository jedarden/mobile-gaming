# Parking Jam / Car Escape / Unblock Puzzle

## Overview

A grid of vehicles pointing in fixed horizontal or vertical directions. A target vehicle (usually red) must exit through a designated opening. Players slide blocking vehicles along their fixed axis — cars can only move forward/backward along their orientation, never rotate — to clear a path. This is a direct digital adaptation of **Rush Hour**, the physical board game invented by Nob Yoshigahara in the 1970s and commercialized by ThinkFun in 1996 (over 1 million units sold).

## Game Mechanics

- **Setup:** A square grid (typically 6×6) with multiple vehicles; one target vehicle, multiple blockers
- **Control:** Tap and drag to slide a vehicle along its fixed axis
- **Constraint:** Vehicles cannot move perpendicularly to their orientation; cannot overlap
- **Win condition:** Target vehicle slides unobstructed to the exit
- **Fail condition:** No valid moves (rare in well-designed levels); some implementations add move limits
- **Difficulty scaling:** More vehicles, more complex interlocking chains, multi-exit puzzles, 3D variants

3D variants (common in mobile) add visual depth and sometimes multiple floors, but the underlying 2D logic is preserved.

## What Makes It Appealing

### The Unraveling Moment
Ads show a grid that looks hopelessly jammed, then one elegant sequence of three or four moves that unravels everything. The visual metaphor of "traffic jam → clear road" is universally legible and broadly resonant. The moment the exit opens and the target vehicle slides out is a textbook satisfaction beat.

### Eureka Satisfaction
The "aha moment" when the solution becomes visible is what game designers call eureka satisfaction — a brief but strong positive affect spike associated with insight problem-solving. Neuroscience research shows insight solutions produce distinctly stronger positive affect than incremental solutions, making this mechanic neurologically differentiated from brute-force puzzle types.

### Broad Demographic Appeal
The physical constraint metaphor (stuck car, blocked traffic) resonates across ages and cultures. The mechanic appeals particularly to an older demographic (35–60) that represents a high-value mobile ad audience — less impulsive about downloads but more willing to spend on games they genuinely enjoy.

### Spatial Reasoning Reward
For players with strong spatial cognition, these puzzles activate a specific kind of flow state. The mechanic engages the brain's planning circuits in a way that feels concrete and physical, more "real" than abstract number puzzles.

## Psychological Principles at Play

| Principle | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Insight satisfaction** | "Aha" solutions produce a neurologically distinct pleasure spike stronger than incremental puzzle-solving |
| **Constraint satisfaction** | Physical lock-and-key puzzles engage spatial planning circuits more deeply than abstract problems |
| **Low frustration ceiling** | The solution space is finite and always discoverable — players rarely feel cheated by an unfair puzzle |
| **Universal metaphor resonance** | "Being stuck in traffic" is a cross-demographic frustration; clearing it satisfies a symbolic as well as mechanical desire |
| **Tactile projection** | Sliding vehicles on a grid maps to physical manipulation instincts — the game feels haptic even on glass |

## Historical Context

**Rush Hour** (ThinkFun, 1996) is the board game equivalent, itself based on Yoshigahara's earlier *Blocked* (1970s). The digital mobile version removes the physical pieces but preserves the core constraint architecture. The mechanic has demonstrated persistent appeal across six decades and multiple form factors, which is unusual in puzzle design history.
